# Trip Generation

## Overview

Trip generation is the first step of the four-step model. It predicts the
number of trips produced by and attracted to each zone, $O_i$ and $D_j$,
from the land use and the socioeconomic characteristics of the zone and its
households. This chapter defines trips and their classification, reviews the
factors that determine trip making, and develops the main model families:
growth factors and trip rates, zonal and household regression models,
cross-classification (category analysis) at household and person level, and
Bayesian updating of transferred rates. It ends with the balancing of
productions and attractions that prepares the input of trip distribution.

:::{admonition} Learning objectives
:class: objectives
After completing this chapter, readers should be able to:

- define trips, tours, home-based and non-home-based trips, and trip
  productions and attractions;
- list the factors that explain trip productions and attractions;
- apply growth-factor and trip-rate models and state their limitations;
- build and assess zonal and household-based regression models, including
  the use of dummy variables and stepwise selection;
- apply household and person cross-classification models;
- update trip rates with Bayesian methods; and
- balance zonal productions and attractions.
:::

## Notation

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $O_i$, $D_j$ | trips produced by zone $i$; trips attracted to zone $j$ |
| $t_i$, $T_i$ | current and future trips of zone $i$ (growth-factor model) |
| $F_i$ | growth factor of zone $i$ |
| $H_i$ | number of households in zone $i$ |
| $t^{(p)}(h)$ | trip rate for purpose $p$ of households of type $h$ |
| $N_i$, $\alpha_{ij}$ | inhabitants of zone $i$; share of them in person category $j$ |
| $A_i$ | accessibility of zone $i$ |

## Definitions and classification of trips

Trip (journey)
: a one-way movement from an origin to a destination. Studies usually
  record all vehicular trips; short walking trips (below a threshold such
  as 300 m) and trips by young children have often been ignored, although
  modern surveys increasingly record non-motorized travel.

Sojourn, activity, and tour
: a *sojourn* is a stay at a location with a purpose (work, study,
  shopping); an *activity* is the endeavor itself; a *tour* (trip chain)
  is a sequence of trips and sojourns starting and ending at the same place,
  usually home. Tours reflect better than trips that travel is a derived
  demand.

Home-based (HB) and non-home-based (NHB) trips
: an HB trip has the home of the traveler at one end (for visitors, the
  hotel); an NHB trip has neither end at home.

Production and attraction
: the **production** is the home end of an HB trip, or the origin of an NHB
  trip; the **attraction** is the non-home end of an HB trip, or the
  destination of an NHB trip ({doc}`06-zoning-and-networks`). **Trip
  generation** usually means the total number of trips generated by the
  households of a zone, HB and NHB.

Trips are classified

- **by purpose**: HB work and HB education (*compulsory* or *mandatory*
  trips), HB shopping, social and recreational, escort, and other HB trips
  (*discretionary* or *optional* trips), and NHB trips. A minimal model
  separates HB work, HB other, and NHB trips;
- **by time of day**: peak and off-peak trips, whose purpose mix differs
  strongly; and
- **by person or household type**: income (e.g., three strata), car
  ownership (0, 1, 2 or more cars), and household size and structure.

## Factors affecting trip generation

**Household productions** depend mainly on income, car ownership, household
size, and household structure (these four are usually collected by
household surveys), and on the value of land, residential density, and
accessibility. Accessibility is rarely included, but it is the only one of
these factors that makes trip generation respond to changes in the transport
system.

**Personal productions** depend on age, gender, income, occupation,
employment status, and car availability.

**Attractions** are explained by the floor space of industrial, commercial,
and service uses and by zonal employment (jobs by sector, school enrollment);
accessibility measures have occasionally been added.

**Freight** productions and attractions — a small share of trips, but an
important contributor to congestion — depend on the number of employees,
sales, and the roofed and total area of firms.

## Growth-factor and trip-rate models

The simplest model scales the current trips $t_i$ of zone $i$ by a growth
factor $F_i$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_i &= F_i\, t_i, \\
F_i &= \frac{f\!\left(P_i^{d}, I_i^{d}, C_i^{d}\right)}{f\!\left(P_i^{c}, I_i^{c}, C_i^{c}\right)},
\end{aligned}
$$ (eq-09-growth-factor-tg)

where $P$, $I$, and $C$ are population, income, and car ownership, and the
superscripts $d$ and $c$ denote the design (future) and current years. The
function $f$ is often a simple product without parameters. A **trip-rate
model** is the special case in which trips are proportional to a single
variable, for example residents: the current rate (trips per resident) is
applied to the future population.

Both assume that the relationship between trips and the explanatory
variables does not change, and both are crude. Growth factors are used in
practice mainly for **external trips** (which are few, so errors stay
small, and are hard to model otherwise) and as a sense check, for example in
inter-urban toll-road studies ({prf:ref}`ex-24-tg-growth-factor`).

## Regression models

### Zonal regression models

A zonal model relates the trips of zone $i$ to zonal totals or averages:

$$
y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_k x_{ik} + \varepsilon_i .
$$ (eq-09-zonal-regression)

Several issues must be considered.

1. **What zonal models can explain.** They capture only the variation
   *between* zones. They work if zones are internally homogeneous and cover
   a wide range of conditions; but most of the variability of trip making is
   between households *within* zones, and a zonal model cannot represent it.
2. **The intercept.** One expects a zone with no households to produce no
   trips, so the regression should pass close to the origin. A large
   intercept (compared with the product of the mean of a variable and its
   coefficient) is a reason to reject the model; an intercept that is not
   significantly different from zero suggests re-estimating the model
   through the origin.
3. **Null zones.** Zones that cannot produce a given trip type (no HB trips
   from non-residential zones) must be excluded, or they inflate the
   apparent fit.
4. **Totals or rates.** The model can be written with zonal totals
   ($y_i$ = trips of zone $i$, $x_{ik}$ = cars of zone $i$) or with rates
   per household, $y_i' = y_i/H_i$ and $x_{ik}' = x_{ik}/H_i$. In the first
   case the error variance grows with zone size; in the second it decreases
   with it — the constant-variance assumption can hold in at most one form.
   Totals also tend to give misleadingly high $R^2$ values, because large
   zones have both many trips and many households.

### Household-based regression models

Smaller zones reduce intra-zonal variation but increase data and computing
costs and sampling error. The alternative is to estimate models at the
**household level**, independent of zone boundaries: each surveyed household
is an observation, so the full range of variability enters the model. The
zonal totals are then obtained by **aggregation**: for a linear model,
multiply the model evaluated at the zonal averages by the number of
households, or sum the predictions over household types
({prf:ref}`ex-24-tg-household-regression`).

### Non-linearity and dummy variables

Linear relationships may hide non-linear effects, especially of qualitative
variables (dwelling type, occupation, age, gender). Two remedies are

1. **transforming** the dependent or independent variables (logarithms,
   powers) to linearize the relationship; and
2. replacing a variable by **dummy variables**. For car ownership, let
   $Z_1 = 1$ for households with exactly one car and $Z_2 = 1$ for
   households with two or more cars (both 0 for households without a car).
   The model $Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 Z_1 + \beta_3 Z_2$ allows
   the second car to have a different effect from the first; graphically it
   gives three parallel lines, one per car-ownership level
   ({prf:ref}`ex-24-tg-stepwise-household`).

### Model selection

A trip generation model is judged by four questions:

1. Is $R^2$ (or better, $\bar{R}^2$ or the residual standard error)
   reasonable?
2. Do the coefficients have the **correct signs** and plausible magnitudes?
3. Are the coefficients **statistically significant**?
4. Is the **intercept** small compared with the effects of the variables?

A **stepwise** procedure organizes the search:

1. examine the relationship between the dependent variable and each
   candidate variable, and linearize non-linear relationships;
2. compute the correlation matrix of all variables;
3. identify the variables that are associated with the dependent variable
   and the pairs of independent variables that are strongly correlated with
   each other (sources of **collinearity**);
4. of any two highly correlated independent variables, keep only one;
5. estimate the candidate models; and
6. assess each candidate by logic and by statistics, and choose.

Statistical packages automate the search (forward selection, backward
elimination, best subsets with Mallows' $C_p$), but the logical checks
remain the analyst's responsibility. Models should also be **validated** on
data not used in estimation, comparing observed and modeled trip rates for
groups of households rather than only totals, because errors of opposite
sign can cancel in totals.

## Cross-classification models

### Household category analysis

A **cross-classification** (category analysis) model, introduced by
Wootton and Pick (1967), assumes that the trip rate of a household type is
stable over time. Households are classified by
a few variables — for example $m$ household sizes and $n$ car-ownership
levels, giving $m \times n$ types $h$ — and the rate of each type is
estimated as

$$
t^{(p)}(h) = \frac{T^{(p)}(h)}{H(h)},
$$ (eq-09-cross-class-rate)

where $T^{(p)}(h)$ is the number of observed trips for purpose $p$ made by
the $H(h)$ sampled households of type $h$. The zonal productions for the
planning year are

$$
O_i^{(p)} = \sum_{h} H_i(h)\, t^{(p)}(h),
$$ (eq-09-cross-class-production)

where $H_i(h)$ is the forecast number of households of type $h$ in zone
$i$. The art lies in choosing categories that minimize the variance of trip
rates within each cell.

*Advantages:* the classes are independent of the zoning system; no
functional form is assumed (relationships need not be linear or even
monotonic); and the effect of one variable may differ between classes of
another.

*Disadvantages:* no extrapolation beyond the calibration classes (the top
classes are usually open-ended, e.g. "2 or more cars"); no statistical
goodness-of-fit measures; large samples are needed because cells must
contain enough households; there is no objective way to choose the
classifying variables and their groupings; and adding a variable multiplies
the number of cells.

Generic trip rates published by agencies (for example the Institute of
Transportation Engineers' *Trip Generation Manual*, or planning authorities'
rates for development control) are cross-classification models applied to
land uses ({prf:ref}`ex-24-tg-attraction-rates`). Cross-classification and
regression can also be combined: households are first stratified (say by
income and car ownership) and a regression on household size and structure
is estimated within each stratum.

### Person category analysis

Household categories do not capture individual characteristics such as age,
gender, or employment status. A **person-category** model classifies
individuals instead. With $t_j$ the trip rate of category $j$, $N_i$ the
inhabitants of zone $i$, and $\alpha_{ij}$ the share of them in category
$j$,

$$
T_i = \sum_{j} N_i\, \alpha_{ij}\, t_j ,
$$ (eq-09-person-category)

separately for HB and NHB trips and by purpose if required
({prf:ref}`ex-24-tg-person-category`).

Fully crossing many variables quickly becomes impractical: gender (2), age
(4), car ownership (3), employment status (2), income (3), race (2),
employment type (3), and family type (5) define
$2 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 3 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5 = 4\,320$
categories. Categories are therefore merged when their trip rates do not
differ significantly (tested with the two-sample test of
{doc}`02-regression-and-hypothesis-tests`), or when the regression
$t_j^{(p)} = \beta_{jk} + \alpha_{jk} t_k^{(p)}$ of the rates of category
$j$ on those of $k$ across purposes satisfies, as suggested by Supernak et
al. (1983),

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{jk} &> 0.90, \\
0.75 &< \alpha_{jk} < 1.25, \\
\beta_{jk} &< 0.10 .
\end{aligned}
$$

*Advantages:* person categories are compatible with the rest of the model
system, which deals with trip makers; they use all relevant variables in a
manageable number of classes; they need smaller samples than household
categories; and they are easier to forecast, since forecasts of person
characteristics need only demographic (survival and migration) models,
whereas household types also need forecasts of household formation.
*Limitation:* interactions within the household and household budgets are
hard to represent.

### Trip generation and accessibility

In the models above, trip generation does not react to changes in the
transport system. Replacing $O_i^n = f(H_i^n)$ by $O_i^n = f(H_i^n, A_i^n)$,
where $A_i^n$ is the accessibility of zone $i$ for person type $n$, makes it
elastic. A common accessibility measure is

$$
A_i^n = \sum_{j} E_j^n \exp(-\beta\, c_{ij}),
$$ (eq-09-accessibility)

where $E_j^n$ measures the attractiveness of zone $j$ (e.g., jobs),
$c_{ij}$ is the generalized cost of travel from $i$ to $j$, and $\beta$ is
the parameter of the gravity model ({doc}`10-trip-distribution`). This
exponential form is a variant of the measure introduced by Hansen (1959).

## Bayesian updating of trip rates

When a model is transferred from another area or an earlier study, it can
be updated with a small local sample. Let the prior (old) rate of a
category have mean $t_1$, variance $S_1^2$, and $n_1$ observations, and the
new sample mean $t_s$, variance $S_s^2$, and $n_s$ observations. The
variances of the two means are $\sigma_1^2 = S_1^2/n_1$ and
$\sigma_s^2 = S_s^2/n_s$. Treating the prior and the sample as independent
normal pieces of information, Bayes' theorem gives a posterior mean that is
the precision-weighted average of the two, with precisions adding:

$$
\begin{aligned}
t_2 &= \frac{t_1/\sigma_1^2 + t_s/\sigma_s^2}{1/\sigma_1^2 + 1/\sigma_s^2} \\
&= \frac{n_1 S_s^2\, t_1 + n_s S_1^2\, t_s}{n_1 S_s^2 + n_s S_1^2}, \\
\sigma_2^2 &= \frac{1}{1/\sigma_1^2 + 1/\sigma_s^2} \\
&= \frac{S_1^2 S_s^2}{n_1 S_s^2 + n_s S_1^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$ (eq-09-bayes-update)

The more precise source dominates, and the posterior is always more precise
than either source ({prf:ref}`ex-24-tg-bayes`). Updating is a natural part of
the continuous planning process of {doc}`05-transportation-planning-process`.

## Balancing productions and attractions

Productions and attractions are estimated by different models, so in
general

$$
\sum_{i} O_i \ne \sum_{j} D_j .
$$

Productions, based on household data, are usually considered more reliable,
so the total is taken as $T = \sum_i O_i$ and the attractions are scaled by

$$
\begin{aligned}
f &= \frac{T}{\sum_j D_j}, \\
D_j' &= f\, D_j,
\end{aligned}
$$ (eq-09-balance-attractions)

so that $\sum_j D_j' = T$. The balanced trip ends $O_i$ and $D_j'$ are the
input of the doubly constrained distribution models of
{doc}`10-trip-distribution`.

## Summary and Common Mistakes

### Summary

- Trips are classified by purpose, time of day, and person or household
  type; HB trips are produced at home, whatever the direction of travel.
- Growth-factor and trip-rate models {eq}`eq-09-growth-factor-tg` are simple
  but assume fixed relationships; they are mainly used for external trips.
- Zonal regression models {eq}`eq-09-zonal-regression` capture only the
  variation between zones; models estimated at the household level use the
  full variability, dummy variables capture non-linear effects, and models
  are chosen by logic and statistics together.
- Cross-classification models apply cell rates {eq}`eq-09-cross-class-rate`
  to forecast numbers of households {eq}`eq-09-cross-class-production` or
  persons {eq}`eq-09-person-category`; person categories are easier to
  forecast.
- Accessibility {eq}`eq-09-accessibility` is the variable that makes trip
  generation respond to the transport system.
- Bayesian updating {eq}`eq-09-bayes-update` combines old rates and new
  samples by their precision.
- Attractions are scaled to the total of productions
  {eq}`eq-09-balance-attractions` before distribution.

### Common mistakes

- **Treating productions and attractions as origins and destinations.** The
  production of an HB trip is always the home end, also for the return trip
  home; only NHB productions are origins.
- **Judging a zonal model fitted on totals by its high $R^2$.** Large zones
  have both many households and many trips, so totals give a high $R^2$
  whatever the quality of the model; compare residual standard errors, or
  fit rates or household-level models.
- **Keeping a large intercept.** It implies trips from zones without
  households; re-estimate through the origin or revise the specification.
- **Using a cross-classification model outside its calibration classes.**
  The model cannot extrapolate; open-ended top classes ("2 or more cars")
  hide changes within them.
- **Forgetting to balance productions and attractions before
  distribution.** A doubly constrained model then has no solution, because
  the row and column totals differ.
